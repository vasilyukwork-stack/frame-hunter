How to check a Trip 35 before you buy

The one test that matters is the red flag. Cover the light meter (the bubbly ring around the lens) with your hand and half-press the shutter: a red flag should pop up in the viewfinder. In bright light it shouldn't. If the flag never appears, the selenium light meter is probably dead and the camera won't expose properly. Look for "red flag works" or "red flag OK" in the listing, or ask the seller.

Listings to skip (or read very carefully)

"No red flag" and "meter not working" from €28. The cheapest UK Trips we found say the red flag doesn't show or the meter is dead. That's a repair job, not a bargain. Same for listings marked "faulty", "for parts" or "untested".

Other Olympus "Trips". The Trip 500, Trip 100, Trip MD2 and MD3, Trip Junior, Supertrip, Trip 601 and AF panorama models are later plastic autofocus or fixed-focus cameras (€40–€99). Fine snapshot cameras, but not a Trip 35.

One Japanese seller, dozens of listings. About two thirds of the Trip 35s we found on eBay Europe come from Japan. One seller alone lists 29 near-identical black Trips at €98–€119 in five languages. Japanese cameras are often in great shape, but the same camera costs about €50–€85 in the UK.

Custom-coloured "MINT" Trips. Pink, green, blue and purple Trips (€140–€190) have been recovered with new leatherette. Olympus never made them in these colours. Nothing wrong with that if you like the look, but you pay double for the covering.

"Selection" listings. A German seller lists a "Sucherkamera Auswahl" (choose one) with the Trip 35 next to Pen cameras at €97–€136. Check which camera you'll actually get.

Wild prices. Some EU sellers ask €230–€245 for a working Trip, and one Japanese "new in box" listing is €337. A serviced one with a warranty costs about €143.
